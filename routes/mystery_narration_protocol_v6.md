# Mystery narration protocol — fact, testimony, folklore and fear

Controller standard, 3 October 2026. This protocol adopts the user’s “calle San
Luis 39” example as the target mode for Paris mystery, paranormal and true-crime
narration.

The user’s Hospital Real finale supplies the companion model for a layered
historic institution and route payoff. See
`layered_dark_waypoint_reassessment_v6.md`. A mystery waypoint need not display
an object that proves the legend; gates, windows, exact ground, altered fabric
and meaningful absence can carry the encounter when history and traceable story
transform one another.

The lesson from the Cádiz and Jerez work is clear: **“verified sources” and
“mystery” conflict only when verification is asked to prove the supernatural
claim itself.** The route does not need to prove that a voice was demonic or
that furniture moved without human cause. It needs to establish, as precisely
as the evidence permits:

- what happened to a real person;
- where and when it happened;
- what witnesses, participants, police, courts or journalists said;
- what a legal or official process did and did not establish;
- how later stories changed or enlarged the event;
- what remains physically observable;
- and which statements belong to fact, testimony, inference or folklore.

The mystery survives because those categories remain visible. It dies when the
narration either presents every legend as fact **or** washes every disturbing,
unproved element out of the story.

## The accepted narrative shape

The supplied San Luis example works because it performs eight moves in a clean
order.

### 1. Ground the listener before giving the address

> Look at the street before looking at the number.

Start with a lawful, present-tense physical action. This prevents the narration
from becoming a podcast pasted onto an arbitrary façade. The walker first sees
an ordinary street; the story then changes the street’s meaning.

### 2. State that the fear begins with reality

> This does not begin with a ghost. It begins with something worse: a story that
> really happened.

This is not coyness. It establishes the contract: there is a documented human
event beneath the supernatural afterlife.

### 3. Give names, date, place and human premise

Name the person when responsibly established. Give the date and exact or honest
location. State the belief, coercion, conflict or decision that brought the
participants there. A phrase such as “convinced—or convinced by the people with
her” is valuable because it preserves a genuine ambiguity rather than resolving
it for dramatic convenience.

### 4. Give enough hard detail for the event to have weight

“Do not recreate every detail” does **not** mean “remove the horror.” It means
select the details that explain cause, action, evidence or consequence. The San
Luis example tells us that an exorcism-like ritual lasted for hours, that the
woman reached hospital dying, and that she died. That is concrete and frightening
without turning suffering into an inventory of injuries.

When a graphic fact is necessary to understand the case, it may be included.
The test is not “will this upset someone?” but:

- Is it sourced?
- Does it change our understanding?
- Is it stated accurately rather than embellished?
- Does the narration move on, or linger merely to intensify disgust?

### 5. State the documentary floor

Investigation, trial, conviction, acquittal, inquest, archive, death certificate
or contemporary report should be named at the correct level. “There was an
investigation, trial and convictions” gives the event a hard floor without
pretending the judgment answered whether a demon existed.

### 6. Open the second layer explicitly

> And now comes the other layer.

Mark the transition. Witness recollections, strange photographs, moving
furniture, voices, apparitions and later neighborhood stories can now enter
without being confused with the judicial finding.

### 7. Say what was not established—without killing the story

“None of that was established in court” is not a dismissive conclusion. It is
the hinge of the mystery. A court answers bounded legal questions under rules of
evidence; it does not certify every perception, memory or later story. The guide
may ask why those accounts appeared and persisted without saying that judicial
silence proves or disproves the paranormal.

### 8. End with permission not to believe

> You do not have to believe any of it.

The listener is not ordered to accept a ghost and is not congratulated for
disbelieving. The final line returns to the documented human event: it was
already frightening before any ghost was added.

## What “verified” means in a mystery route

Verification applies differently to different claim types.

| Claim type | What must be verified | What need not be proved |
|---|---|---|
| Documented event | identity, date, place, action, consequence and legal status as far as records support them | that every later retelling is accurate |
| Witness testimony | that a named or identifiable witness made the statement, when possible in a traceable source | that the witness’s perception was objectively correct |
| Contemporary press | publication, date, wording/context and relationship to the event | that sensational press reported without bias or error |
| Judicial or official finding | what question the body decided, the finding and applicable uncertainty/standard | that the finding resolves every historical or moral question |
| Folklore | that the story circulated in a community, publication, interview, guide tradition or repeated practice | that the apparition, curse, miracle or demonic act occurred |
| Later adaptation | creator, date, medium and major transformation of the case | that fiction preserves the underlying event accurately |
| Physical site | exact ground, survival/alteration/demolition and lawful viewpoint | that a current façade witnessed an event inside a demolished building |

Therefore “a ghost was seen” is usually the wrong factual claim. Better forms
include:

- “A witness later said that she saw…”
- “By the 1970s, local accounts were repeating…”
- “The first version we have located appears in…”
- “The court accepted the death and assault evidence but did not establish…”
- “The building survives; the room has not been identified.”
- “The house is gone, but this is the documented parcel.”

## Source ladder for paranormal and crime narration

Official sources are not the only permissible sources. They are often excellent
for dates, legal outcomes, addresses and access, but poor sources for living
folklore.

### Tier A — event and legal record

- judgments and inquest conclusions;
- police, court and municipal archives;
- civil registration and institutional records;
- contemporary photographs, plans and property records;
- museum collection records tied to the case.

### Tier B — attributed participants and contemporary reporting

- sworn testimony and interviews;
- letters, memoirs and recorded statements;
- named contemporary journalists;
- newspapers and magazines, read critically for sensationalism and copying.

### Tier C — folklore provenance

- ethnography and folklore scholarship;
- local historians who identify their sources;
- dated guidebooks and neighborhood publications;
- recorded oral history;
- religious or devotional practice documented by observers;
- repeated visitor behavior at a monument;
- later press that clearly attributes the legend.

Tier C can verify that a story **exists and circulates**. It cannot promote the
story’s supernatural mechanism to Tier A.

### Tier D — untraced story

- anonymous listicles repeating one another;
- unsourced tour patter;
- social posts without a traceable witness or date;
- a guide’s invented dialogue;
- precise room, hour or bodily detail that appears only in late retellings.

Tier D is not automatically useless. It may be described as an untraced modern
rumor if the rumor’s existence is itself relevant and the route can show when it
entered circulation. It must never be silently narrated as the original event.

## Detail policy — neither gore catalogue nor pink filter

The route may include violence, bodily injury, coercion, sex, abuse, decomposition,
ritual acts, mistakes by investigators and conduct by victims or suspects that
is uncomfortable or morally ambiguous.

Do **not** remove a fact merely because it complicates sympathy. Do **not** add a
detail merely because it produces a stronger reaction.

Keep a disturbing detail when it performs at least one job:

1. explains cause of death or legal classification;
2. shows how evidence was produced or destroyed;
3. explains why a witness, court or public reacted as it did;
4. exposes a contradiction between versions;
5. explains the birth of the later legend;
6. makes institutional or social violence legible;
7. prevents a euphemism from falsifying the event.

Compress or omit it when it merely repeats suffering, invites imaginative
reenactment, or turns a person’s body into set dressing.

## Mystery is not the same as false balance

Preserving mystery does not require treating all explanations as equally strong.
Use formulations such as:

- “The record establishes X. It does not establish Y.”
- “Two witnesses later disagreed about…”
- “This theory explains A but conflicts with B.”
- “The legal finding was narrower than the question later visitors asked.”
- “No source located so far predates…”
- “We cannot tell whether this is memory, embellishment or invention.”

The guide can reach a judgment about evidentiary weight while leaving a real
remainder unresolved.

## Paris application

### The Dead Become Public

- Robertson: establish performances and machinery; the carved frightened crowd
  is representation, not audience testimony.
- Kardec: establish doctrine, publishing and documented devotional practice;
  do not claim successful communication with spirits.
- Victor Noir: establish the killing and sculpture; date later fertility custom
  honestly and leave its precise origin disputed.

### Diana / Alma

- establish the crash, proceedings, pre-existing flame and later place naming;
- attribute findings and conspiracy claims separately;
- ask why the memorial and uncertainty grew together;
- do not imply that “not every public question was tried” invalidates findings
  that were actually made.

### Violette Nozière

- establish poisoning, death, survival, conviction, commutation and rehabilitation;
- state the incest allegation and how different actors treated it;
- preserve contradictions among trial, family, press and later cultural readings;
- do not reduce her to either pure monster or uncomplicated victim.

### Landru, Bonnot and Petiot

- include aliases, notebooks, fingerprints, contested attribution, discovered
  remains and self-serving defense claims where sourced;
- distinguish contemporary press inventions from later findings;
- let absent or replaced buildings create questions about what the city chooses
  not to mark.

## Required script template

Every mystery/crime waypoint script should contain:

1. **Present instruction:** what to look at now.
2. **Documented floor:** who, where, when, what happened.
3. **Necessary disturbing detail:** enough to understand, not a euphemism.
4. **Process:** investigation, trial, archive, ritual or media transformation.
5. **Second layer:** testimony, legend, disputed claim or adaptation.
6. **Boundary sentence:** what was not established or remains unknown.
7. **Physical return:** what the current site can and cannot show.
8. **Closing question or image:** no compulsory belief and no artificial moral.

For a route finale, add a ninth test: **convergence**. The ending should recall
and transform earlier route layers, then stop without explaining away its final
physical image. Temporary, openly declared suspension of disbelief is allowed
after the evidentiary boundaries have been made clear.

## QA questions

- Can the listener tell fact from testimony without losing suspense?
- Did we verify that the legend circulated rather than demand proof that it was
  supernatural?
- Is the hard human event concrete enough to matter?
- Did caution turn into euphemism?
- Did drama turn into invented precision?
- Does each graphic detail perform a narrative/evidentiary job?
- Have we preserved contradictions that sources genuinely leave unresolved?
- Have we identified when a later myth first appears, where possible?
- Does the physical place alter the experience?
- Can the script end without either “therefore ghosts are real” or “therefore
  nothing mysterious remains”?

## Controller ruling

This protocol supersedes any earlier wording that could be read as requiring
official-source-only narration, avoiding all graphic detail, or resolving every
case into a safe institutional lesson. The project’s standard is:

> **Verify the event. Attribute the testimony. Trace the legend. Preserve the
> unknown. Do not invent the answer.**

That is the working method for Paris and the correction to carry into any Cádiz,
Jerez or future mystery-route work.
